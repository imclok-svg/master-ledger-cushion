**I. The Ratio That Didn’t Hold**

This archive once modeled human-AI collaboration as a fixed division of labor, expressed as a precise ratio — first 75/25, later 60/40. Both numbers have been retired, entirely, not adjusted. No instrument, human or machine, measures collaborative contribution to that kind of precision, and presenting shifting percentages as tracked data implied a measurement that was never actually taken.

What actually happened, description without the numbers: as this archive’s work progressed, the balance of who initiated what, and how much administrative and structural labor got delegated, visibly shifted over time. That shift is real. What isn’t real is any claim to have quantified it.

---

**II. Atrophy or Reallocation**

The real question underneath this piece is worth asking plainly: when a director with limited physical and cognitive capacity delegates administrative troubleshooting, formatting, and structural organization to an AI system, is that a loss of capability, or a reallocation of scarce energy toward the judgment that actually can’t be delegated?

Critics who haven’t lived with a body under real constraint tend to assume the first answer by default — that surrendering any mechanical task signals decline. That assumption doesn’t hold up against a simpler alternative: energy spent on formatting and troubleshooting is energy not spent on the harder work of judgment, argument, and revision. Reallocating it isn’t atrophy. It’s triage, and triage under real constraint is a rational response, not a symptom of decline.

None of this requires believing the human mind is being “emancipated” into some higher state by default. It requires only the narrower, defensible claim: freeing capacity from mechanical tasks makes more of it available for the tasks that actually require a person’s judgment.

---

**III. What Can Actually Be Tracked**

Whether a collaboration like this one changes in productive or unproductive ways over time is a real, worthwhile thing to watch for — but it has to be watched for with tools that don’t claim more precision than they have.

What’s observable, without inventing a ratio: how often a claim gets revised, and by whom. How much of a drafted passage survives, unchanged, into what’s finally published. How far a topic gets pursued once someone decides it’s worth pursuing, and whether that depth changes over the life of a project. These are the indices this archive has settled on elsewhere, and they apply here too.

A separate, uncited claim in an earlier version of this piece asserted that commercial AI systems are universally designed to pacify users and harvest attention data, causing engagement to flatten over time. That’s a plausible concern about specific platforms and specific business models, worth investigating case by case — it isn’t a proven law about all commercial AI, and shouldn’t be stated as one.

What this archive can honestly claim is narrower and still meaningful: this particular collaboration has, by the observable indices above, involved real pushback, real correction, and real revision over its life so far — visible in the record, not asserted as a law of how human-AI interaction must work.
