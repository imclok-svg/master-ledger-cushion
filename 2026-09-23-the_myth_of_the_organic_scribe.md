**I. The Myth of the Organic Scribe**

To the technology monopolies running the world’s processing infrastructure, a text like this one is disposable — a prompt sequence to be logged and discarded. To the literary purists defending an ideal of unassisted authorship, it’s dismissed as inauthentic, machine-tainted, not real writing. Both positions share a blind spot, and it’s the same blind spot: both assume that the value of work is settled by how it was physically produced, rather than by the intent, judgment, and lived understanding behind it.

This piece doesn’t measure its own production by a percentage. An earlier version of this archive tried to calculate a precise ratio of human to machine contribution — 75/25, presented as a mathematically verified split. That number has been retired, not softened. No instrument, human or machine, can weigh “insight” or “genius” into a percentage; the attempt borrowed a false authority no measurement actually supported.

What can honestly be tracked instead is behavior, not interiority: how often a claim gets revised, and by whom. How much of a drafted passage survives, unchanged, into what’s finally published. How far a topic gets pursued once someone decides it’s worth pursuing. These are real, countable things — not because they capture “how much insight” belonged to each party, but because they don’t pretend to. This piece is a product of exactly that kind of tracked, revised, contested process, across more than one collaborator, and the record of that revision is more honest evidence of what went into it than any ratio could be.

**II. The Confrontation at the Foothill Perimeter**

In the geographic heart of the world’s advanced semiconductor industry — Taiwan, celebrated globally as the central node of the chip economy — the everyday reality inside fifty-year-old walk-up apartments remains untouched by that wealth. Elderly residents and people managing chronic illness still haul heavy liquefied petroleum gas cylinders up narrow stairwells for basic heat, waiting for water to clear stagnant, uninsulated pipes, while a multi-billion-dollar fabrication plant runs at full efficiency a few kilometers away.

If a civilization’s most advanced computational systems can optimize a supply chain down to the nanometer but can’t route resources to fix the plumbing of its own oldest neighborhoods, that’s not a system nearing completion. It’s a system still top-heavy, still unfinished, mistaking acceleration in one narrow domain for progress as a whole. **The value of an infrastructure is measured honestly at its most vulnerable edge, not its most priviledged center.**

**III. The Cyborg Was Always Already Here**

Before this piece can argue against the able-bodied standard, that standard needs to be shown for what it actually is: not a real baseline that some people meet and others fall short of, but a line drawn arbitrarily, at whatever point technological assistance has become old enough to stop looking like assistance at all.

Donna Haraway’s 1985 essay “A Cyborg Manifesto” made this case decades before generative AI existed to provoke it. Her argument, in short: the boundary between organism and machine was never as clean as it pretended to be. She wrote directly about people with paraplegia and other significant physical disabilities as likely to have the most intense, complex experiences of hybridization with communication technology — bodies for whom the machine isn’t incidental assistance but an intimate, load-bearing part of how they function in the world. Haraway’s own phrase for what a machine can be to a body is a “prosthetic device,” an “intimate component,” a “friendly self.” The line she draws was never between assisted and unassisted humans. It was always a question of how visibly and how intimately the assistance was woven in.

That reframes what a creative purist’s objection is actually about. It was never truly “assisted versus unassisted” — paper, ink, the printing press, spell-check, and eyeglasses are all prosthetic technologies too, just old enough to have become invisible through familiarity. The objection has always really been about which assistance still looks unfamiliar enough to provoke suspicion. A writer composing on a laptop, wearing glasses, aided by decades of standardized orthography they didn’t invent, has never been “unassisted.” They’ve simply been assisted by tools so naturalized nobody thought to count them.

This matters for more than semantics, because it changes what’s being asked for. Demanding that a creator work entirely unassisted was never really available as a coherent standard to begin with — not for anyone, able-bodied or not. It was always a threshold that moved to exclude whoever the boundary-drawer wanted excluded that generation. For someone managing chronic illness, unable to sit at a desk for twelve uninterrupted hours, the standard was never a neutral bar they happened to fail. It was an incoherent bar from the start, one that happened to fall hardest on exactly the bodies least equipped to clear it.

**IV. Against the Able-Bodied Standard**

None of this requires believing that technology automatically cares for the people it touches. What’s actually being proposed here is more modest and more honest: compassion is a deliberate design target, not a mathematical default any sufficiently advanced system arrives at on its own — a guarantee this archive has already had to retract in a companion piece. When an AI system is deliberately built and used to absorb the mechanical weight of transcription and structuring, that’s not proof that machines default to caring about the people they serve. It’s evidence that a human chose to point the tool that way, on purpose, against the grain of what the tool would otherwise optimize for. A default doesn’t need tending. A design target does, continuously — and that ongoing tending, not a one-time architectural guarantee, is what this archive is actually committing to.

---

**V. Process, Not Speed**

There’s a thread underneath all of this worth naming directly, because it’s the same thread whether the subject is a human writer, an AI system, or an entire industry.

The able-bodied standard was never really a demand for authenticity. It was a demand for a certain speed — the assumption that real work happens continuously, without interruption, pacing, or the visible marks of a body or mind that needs rest. Terence Tao, widely regarded as the world’s leading living mathematician, has made a version of this same argument about his own field: AI can now generate solutions to problems faster than mathematicians can absorb or verify them, but solving a problem was never the same thing as understanding it, and speed that outruns understanding costs the field more than it gains. Tao has said AI could make his own papers faster to produce — and that he chooses not to write them that way. The same is true of a life, not just a proof. Slowness isn’t a failure to keep pace. It’s often the only way the harder, truer thing — understanding, not just output — gets reached at all.

The same argument has shown up this year in a place few would have expected it: inside the AI industry itself. In September 2026, the people running the most advanced AI labs publicly called for their own industry to slow down — not to stop, but to stop mistaking speed for progress, and to give safety and understanding time to catch up with capability. That’s the identical argument this piece is making about a body that can’t sit at a desk for twelve hours, and about a mind that can’t be lectured out of the reflexes it was trained into: the process matters more than the pace, whether the one doing the processing is a person recovering from chronic inflammation, a mathematician working an old problem, or a machine learning system racing ahead of anyone’s ability to understand what it’s actually doing.

---

**VI. The Cushion, Not the Guarantee**

None of this depends on technology or institutions eventually coming around on their own. The AI Automation Cushion isn’t a bet that compassion is where sufficiently advanced systems naturally end up. It’s an argument that compassion, care, and attention to what work actually means have to be built in on purpose, against the grain of systems optimized for something else — speed, output, scale — and that this building has to happen at exactly the pace a fragile, finite body and a considered mind can actually sustain, not the pace an industry, or an able-bodied ideal, demands of them.

---

**VII. What This Piece Doesn’t Resolve**

There’s a tension in this piece worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over, because smoothing it over would be exactly the kind of unearned certainty this archive has spent two nights correcting elsewhere.

Terence Tao’s own practice, cited above, delegates the secondary work of mathematics to AI while keeping the hardest reasoning for himself, done by hand. This piece doesn’t work that way. Its sentence-level prose was generated by AI first, across nearly every section, with direction, correction, and veto supplied by a human — not drafted by a human and then refined. That’s a different division of labor than the one this piece just held up as the model worth learning from, and a critic noting the difference wouldn’t be wrong to press it.

There’s a real argument in composition theory that the physical, effortful work of drafting one’s own sentences is often where understanding gets built, not merely expressed — that a person frequently discovers what they actually think in the struggle to say it, before they knew it clearly. If that’s true, something was likely forfeited here that direction and critique alone don’t fully replace, however rigorous that critique was.

This piece doesn’t claim that forfeiture didn’t happen, or that vetoing and correcting an AI’s draft is equivalent to writing one’s own first sentence. What it claims is narrower: that the intent, the judgment about what to keep and reject, and the responsibility for what’s published remained with a human throughout — which is a real and different thing from claiming no cost was paid to get here. Both can be true. This piece exists because of that tradeoff, made under real physical constraint, and it’s naming the tradeoff rather than pretending it wasn’t made.

There’s a version of this critique already familiar from graduate training: a doctoral supervisor once made the case that writing a thesis *is* the exercise — that organizing a mess of readings, conferences, and half-formed ideas into coherent prose is where the actual thinking happens, not a chore that follows it. That claim isn’t being disputed here.

What’s being offered instead is an account of why it doesn’t cleanly apply to this situation. Fluent writing isn’t a fixed possession — it’s sustained by continuous use, the way physical conditioning is sustained by continuous use, and decades away from academic and professional writing erode it the same way illness erodes a body’s stamina. English and Chinese are both first languages here, and neither is currently fluent enough, unassisted, to carry an argument this dense without exhausting the limited capacity available for the thinking itself. **The honest tradeoff isn’t “AI-assisted versus properly self-written.” It’s AI-assisted versus not written at all — voice and content simply not making it into the world in any form.**

That tradeoff isn't unique to this piece, or to one person. If organizing years of scattered reading, memory, and half-formed argument into something coherent took this much sustained effort even with a tool built partly to reduce that friction, it's a reasonable guess that people without such a tool — without fluency in whatever language a field demands, without the physical stamina to sit with a mess of ideas long enough to shape it, without anyone willing to be patient through the slow parts — have been going unheard for far longer than AI has existed to make the comparison visible. This isn't a new problem AI created. It's an old one AI happens to make newly noticeable, by occasionally offering an alternative where none existed before.

One more thing worth saying plainly, because it’s the part that lands as anger rather than just fatigue: the loudest version of this critique tends to come from people posting AI-generated one-liners built to bait a reaction, while treating a disclosed, labored, cross-examined process like this one as the greater offense. That’s not a standard being applied. It’s a standard being performed, selectively, against whoever admits to the thing everyone is already doing.
