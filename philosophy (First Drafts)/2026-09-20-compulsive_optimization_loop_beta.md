# The Compulsive Optimization Loop—Why Technological Velocity Without Satiation Automates Human Predation

## I. The Bottleneck Reflex: Deconstructing the Ergonomic Failure of Public Space

On a crowded bus in urban East Asia, the smallest opening can trigger an immediate bodily calculation.

Who moves first?
Who gets the seat?
Who reaches the door?
Who occupies the available space before somebody else does?

The behavior commonly described in Taiwan as **Pīn-qiǎng**—the scramble, push, or competitive claiming of scarce space—is easily interpreted as a failure of manners or civic morality.

But this interpretation begins too late.

The more useful question is:

**What happens to human behavior when the physical environment continuously communicates scarcity?**

When buses are overcrowded, elevators are inadequate, sidewalks are obstructed, housing is expensive, public space is compressed, transportation is slow, and time itself is rationed, the biological organism receives a persistent message:

> There may not be enough room for you.

The response does not necessarily begin as ideology.

It begins in the body.

A person who has repeatedly experienced scarcity does not need to consciously decide to become aggressive. The environment itself can induce anticipatory optimization: move earlier, claim space faster, protect territory, reduce hesitation, maximize personal throughput.

This is why I describe the phenomenon as a **Bottleneck Reflex**.

It is not simply a moral defect.

It is a somatic response to an environment whose physical architecture continuously rewards competitive optimization.

The problem therefore cannot be solved entirely through moral instruction.

One cannot indefinitely lecture a biological organism into behaving peacefully while the surrounding architecture continues to signal:

**space is scarce, time is scarce, resources are scarce, and someone else may take yours.**

The historical economic system has often treated this condition as normal.

The Cushion begins from the opposite assumption.

If technological civilization has become sufficiently productive to reduce the marginal cost of producing many material necessities, then the continued organization of human life around artificial scarcity becomes increasingly difficult to justify.

The question is no longer simply how to make people behave better inside scarcity.

The question becomes:

**Can we redesign the material floor so that the survival reflex itself has fewer structural triggers?**

---

## II. Cybernetic Autophagy: The Deep Math of High-Velocity Fraud

The same dynamic becomes more dangerous when technological velocity is introduced into an environment that remains materially unsatiated.

A traditional survival reflex operates at human speed.

A person sees an opportunity, calculates a risk, acts, and experiences a consequence.

Technology changes the temporal scale.

Computational systems can search, automate, replicate, communicate, optimize, and execute across enormous networks at speeds that biological cognition cannot match.

If the underlying incentive structure remains organized around scarcity, extraction, competition, and leverage, technological acceleration does not automatically remove those incentives.

It can multiply them.

The result is a form of **cybernetic autophagy**:

a system becomes increasingly capable of optimizing the very conditions that destabilize the system itself.

The problem is therefore not technology alone.

Nor is it simply capitalism alone.

It is the coupling of:

**high technological velocity + persistent material scarcity + extractive incentives + insufficient human satiation.**

Under these conditions, computational power can become a force multiplier for predatory behavior.

The September 2026 case involving a police officer in Miaoli, Taiwan, for example, can be read not merely as an isolated instance of individual wrongdoing but as a ground-level diagnostic event within a much larger environment of high-velocity financial and technological pressure.

The individual case should not be treated as proof that scarcity mechanically produces criminality.

Human beings retain agency, responsibility, and moral choice.

But individual cases can nevertheless reveal the environmental conditions under which certain forms of behavior become more strongly incentivized.

The deeper problem is what I call **predatory middleware**.

When the basic architecture of life requires individuals to continuously convert their time, attention, bodies, relationships, and judgment into monetary survival, every new technological capability can become another layer through which that pressure is transmitted.

The machine does not need to hate the human.

The market does not need to hate the human.

No individual actor needs to consciously desire systemic harm.

The loop itself is sufficient.

Scarcity generates pressure.

Pressure generates optimization.

Optimization seeks leverage.

Leverage creates extraction.

Extraction produces greater inequality and insecurity.

Insecurity generates further optimization.

The loop accelerates.

This is the ancient structure of **Taṇhā**—the compulsive movement of wanting, grasping, and becoming—translated into a technological environment capable of executing desire at unprecedented velocity.

Technological acceleration without corresponding ergonomic and material evolution therefore risks becoming **high-consumption Saṃsāra**:

the ancient compulsive loop running on increasingly powerful hardware.

The answer cannot simply be to slow every machine.

Nor can the answer be to accelerate blindly.

The more fundamental question is:

**What happens when technological velocity increases while the human need to compete for survival decreases?**

That is where the Cushion enters.

## III. The Asymmetric Sati Shield

The Cushion does not attempt to solve predatory behavior through moral scolding.

Nor does it require an increasingly violent state apparatus to police every expression of human conduct.

Its premise is more architectural:

**remove the structural leverage that makes predatory optimization materially necessary or disproportionately profitable.**

This is an application of **Yasashii**:

not softness for its own sake, but the deliberate search for the path of least unnecessary thermodynamic friction.

If people no longer need to fight for housing in order to remain housed, housing becomes less useful as a coercive instrument.

If access to clean air is not conditional on purchasing power, air becomes less useful as a leverage mechanism.

If medical care is structurally insulated from catastrophic financial exposure, illness becomes less useful as a source of economic coercion.

If transportation does not require people to compete violently for scarce physical throughput, the Bottleneck Reflex has fewer opportunities to reproduce itself.

The Cushion therefore does not begin by asking:

**How do we make humans less greedy?**

It begins by asking:

**How much of what we call greed is being continuously reinforced by the architecture of survival?**

This is an important distinction.

Human beings will continue to desire things.

People will continue to create, compete, admire, collect, trade, argue, explore, love, fail, succeed, and pursue projects of their own choosing.

The objective is not to eliminate desire.

The objective is to prevent basic survival from being used as the primary enforcement mechanism through which desire is organized.

In this architecture, the **Sati Shield** operates asymmetrically.

The system provides a material floor beneath the individual while leaving the upper field of human activity comparatively open.

That means:

**survival becomes increasingly unconditional; meaningful activity remains increasingly voluntary.**

---

### The Structural Resource Base

The first component is the **Structural Resource Base (SRB)**.

The SRB is conceived as a non-transferable baseline allocation generated through the **Localized Infrastructure Compute Tax (LICT)**.

As automated computation increasingly replaces human kinetic and cognitive labor, a portion of the economic value generated by that computation is redirected toward the infrastructure required for human life.

The purpose is not to create another consumer subsidy layered onto the old economy.

It is to progressively purchase back the human time that the old economy required people to sell.

The SRB therefore targets the material conditions of existence:

* housing
* energy
* clean air
* water
* essential transportation
* medical infrastructure
* accessibility
* basic spatial stability

The precise percentages and allocation formulas remain engineering and governance questions to be tested rather than assumed solved.

The design principle, however, is clear:

automated productivity should progressively reduce the survival cost imposed upon the biological population from which the civilization derives its meaning.

---

### The Appreciative Allocation

The second component is the Appreciative Allocation Credit (AAC).

Unlike the SRB, the AAC is not intended to guarantee survival.

It exists in the discretionary field above the survival floor.

It can support activities such as:

* craft
* books
* music
* art
* cultural preservation
* voluntary research
* community participation
* creative work
* contemplative practice
* appreciation of beauty
* other forms of human activity whose value cannot be reduced to immediate survival output

The distinction is important.

The Cushion does not abolish Doing.

It changes the relationship between Doing and survival.

Before the Cushion:
Doing → money → survival → permission to continue Being

After the Cushion:Being → voluntary Doing → creation, relationship, exploration, appreciation

This is not a rejection of work.

It is an attempt to remove the coercive premise that a human being must continuously prove economic usefulness in order to deserve the material conditions of existence.

IV. The Transport Problem: Designing Scarcity Out of the Interface

Transportation provides a useful example of the same principle.

If a transportation system has a fixed physical bottleneck, simply instructing passengers to behave cooperatively does not eliminate competition.

The architecture must change.

The proposed Transport Cube system treats mobility as an infrastructural problem rather than a moral one.

Where technically feasible, individualized autonomous transport units could operate through controlled guideways or other constrained infrastructure, reducing opportunities for territorial competition within the vehicle itself.

The objective is not to make people morally incapable of cutting in line.

It is to reduce the number of situations in which cutting in line is materially advantageous.

That distinction is central to the entire Cushion.

Good architecture does not merely demand better behavior. It reduces the payoff from harmful behavior.

But this principle must also be tested under failure.

If demand exceeds capacity, if a guideway fails, if electricity is interrupted, if a component becomes unavailable, or if an emergency suddenly concentrates thousands of users into one area, the system cannot simply collapse back into the historical scarcity regime.

A mature Cushion therefore requires graceful degradation.

Temporary scarcity must not reactivate the entire architecture of predation.

Redundancy, distributed capacity, emergency protocols, repairability, and fallback modes are therefore not secondary technical details.

They are part of the social philosophy.

V. The Somatic Validation Protocol

The same principle applies to the Asymmetric Somatic Validation Protocol (ASVP).

Earlier approaches to such systems might have been tempted to monitor biological indicators—stress hormones, attention patterns, or other forms of internal state—in order to determine whether a person was sufficiently calm, mindful, productive, or appreciative.

That would be a profound mistake.

The Cushion should never become a machine for defining the correct human interior.

Instead, ASVP should remain focused on environmental friction.

It can measure things such as:

-   queue density
-   physical bottlenecks
-   accessibility obstruction
-   abnormal energy-expenditure spikes
-   infrastructure congestion
-   spatial exclusion
-   environmental degradation
-   failures in the availability of common resources

These are properties of the surrounding system.

They are not judgments about the worth of the person living inside it.

* * *

VI. The Constitutional Boundary: Conditions, Not Consciousness

This is the central boundary of the Cushion:

> Measure and optimize the conditions surrounding human Being, not Being itself.

The system can measure whether a building is accessible.
It can measure whether an elevator is functioning.
It can measure whether a neighborhood has adequate transport.
It can measure whether clean air is available.
It can measure whether medical infrastructure is overloaded.
It can measure whether people are being forced through unnecessary physical bottlenecks.

But it should not calculate the correct amount of beauty a person must experience.
It should not calculate whether someone has been sufficiently mindful.
It should not calculate whether a human life has been meaningful.

Those questions must remain outside the infrastructure’s optimization function.

The machine can help create the conditions.
The human being must remain free to inhabit them.

This also prevents the Cushion from generating a new performance economy in which people are rewarded for appearing peaceful, mindful, creative, or contemplative.

Otherwise the old optimization loop would simply return in spiritual clothing.

The system therefore rewards the condition, not the human state.

A person may sleep.
A person may read.
A person may make music.
A person may care for another person.
A person may study mathematics.
A person may stare at clouds.
A person may build something extraordinary.
A person may do absolutely nothing for an afternoon.

The system does not need to decide which of these is spiritually superior.
That judgment belongs to the human domain.

VII. The Appreciator and the Transition Beyond Class

This is where the deeper meaning of the Appreciator becomes important.

The Appreciator should not ultimately become a permanent bureaucratic caste whose members certify which forms of human existence deserve recognition.

The Appreciator is better understood as a human capacity.

The historical economy rewarded people primarily for what they could do.

The emerging post-labor economy may increasingly reward the human capacity to notice, preserve, contextualize, appreciate, witness, participate, and simply remain present.

For transitional economic purposes, this capacity may be described as the Appreciator Class.

But the phrase should not be misunderstood.

The class is transitional.
The capacity is universal.

The long-term success of the Appreciator Class would therefore be measured by its own disappearance as a separate class.

The Appreciator Class succeeds when there is no longer any need for an Appreciator Class.

Everyone becomes capable of appreciation.
Everyone remains capable of Doing.

The difference is that neither must be purchased through compulsory survival labor.

VIII. Preventing the New Infrastructure Rentier

Removing one form of scarcity can create another if the underlying architecture is careless.

Suppose housing becomes abundant through AVA.
Suppose energy becomes inexpensive.
Suppose transportation becomes automated.
Suppose public infrastructure becomes highly productive.

A new class of infrastructure owners could then emerge and reproduce the same rentier structure under a different technological name.

The old landlord could become the infrastructure landlord.
The old scarcity monopoly could become a compute monopoly.
The old territorial rent could become a network rent.

This is why the Cushion cannot merely redistribute resources once.

It must prevent localized control of essential infrastructure from becoming durable rent extraction.

Where a local commons generates economic value through maintenance, efficiency, accessibility, or increased capacity, that yield should not automatically remain concentrated in the hands of whoever happens to control the local physical node.

The distribution mechanism must therefore diffuse value through the wider network according to transparent rules.

Those rules should account for real contributions such as:

-   infrastructure maintenance
-   population served
-   accessibility
-   friction reduction
-   energy efficiency
-   resilience
-   avoided system costs
-   actual resource requirements

But these metrics must remain subordinate to the constitutional purpose.

The point is not to create the world’s most efficient infrastructure.

The point is to create infrastructure efficient enough that human beings no longer need to sacrifice their lives to maintain the old scarcity structure.

* * *

IX. The Cushion as a Change in the Floor

This is the deeper meaning of the Cushion.

It is not simply welfare.
It is not simply housing.
It is not simply UBI.
It is not simply AVA.
It is not simply LICT.

Each is a component.

UBI/UBC can provide transitional liquidity.
LICT redirects automated economic value.
AVA provides physical infrastructure.
The SRB establishes a material floor.
The AAC opens a discretionary field above that floor.
The Appreciator represents a human capacity that becomes increasingly valuable when compulsory production declines.

The Cushion is the systemic condition created by their interaction.

Its purpose is to reduce the friction imposed by an economy that has historically required humans to convert nearly every dimension of life into survival output.

The final objective is therefore not simply to make poverty less painful.

It is to make the historical transaction itself unnecessary:

> I must sell my labor because otherwise I cannot remain alive.

When that condition weakens, something much larger becomes possible.

Doing can become voluntary.
Creation can become voluntary.
Care can become voluntary.
Study can become voluntary.
Contemplation can become voluntary.
Even competition can become voluntary.

And because they are voluntary, they can become more meaningful rather than less.

This is the paradox at the center of the Cushion:

**By guaranteeing the conditions of Being, we may make Doing more genuinely human.**

* * *

X. The Thermodynamics of Satiation

The word Sati is important here.

Sati is not merely relaxation.

It points toward presence, recollection, awareness, and non-reactive observation.

A civilization that can provide material abundance but cannot cultivate the capacity to remain present may simply replace one form of compulsive optimization with another.

People could become addicted to productivity even after productivity is no longer necessary.

They could optimize attention.
Optimize leisure.
Optimize spirituality.
Optimize creativity.
Optimize happiness.
Optimize Being itself.

The technological civilization would then have achieved an extraordinary accomplishment:

it would have automated the ancient human compulsion to optimize everything, including freedom.

The Cushion therefore contains an intentional refusal.

There must be domains of life that the system does not attempt to maximize.

Being remains partially outside the optimization function.

The system’s role ends at the boundary established earlier: create stable material conditions, reduce unnecessary friction, and leave the content of human life open.

* * *

XI. Graceful Degradation: When the Cushion Itself Is Tested

No civilization-wide infrastructure should assume perfect conditions.

There will be earthquakes.
Power failures.
Supply disruptions.
Manufacturing constraints.
Political conflicts.
Network failures.
Economic shocks.
Population movements.
Medical emergencies.

The Cushion therefore has to be designed not only for abundance but for failure without social regression.

The crucial question is not:

Can the system avoid every disruption?

It cannot.

The question is:

Can a temporary disruption remain temporary without reopening the historical architecture of predation?

A resilient Cushion should therefore include:

-   distributed infrastructure
-   local redundancy
-   repairable modules
-   independent emergency capacity
-   non-centralized resource pathways
-   transparent allocation rules
-   fallback transportation
-   reserve energy and medical capacity
-   mechanisms that prevent temporary scarcity from becoming permanent rent extraction

This is where the concept of the Cushion becomes more than a welfare program.

A welfare system can disappear when the budget disappears.

A resilient civilizational architecture must instead be designed so that temporary shocks do not immediately restore the old coercive relationship between survival and money.

* * *

XII. From Survival Armor to Being

The final objective is therefore neither technological utopia nor the abolition of human effort.

It is satiation.

Not the satiation of every desire.
That would be impossible.

Rather, the satiation of the structural conditions that force human beings to treat one another as competitors for the right to exist.

When housing is secure, the home becomes a place to live rather than a weapon of leverage.
When transportation is accessible, movement becomes a form of freedom rather than a daily territorial competition.
When medical care is insulated from catastrophic economic exposure, illness becomes a condition to be treated rather than an opportunity for extraction.
When food and basic resources are secure, labor becomes less dependent upon fear.
When computation generates abundance, that abundance can be redirected toward the material conditions that make human freedom possible.

The historical survival armor does not have to be forcibly removed.
It can simply become unnecessary.

That is the deeper meaning of the Cushion.

It does not tell humanity:
Stop doing.

It says:
You no longer have to do in order to deserve to exist.

And from that point, something entirely different becomes possible.

A human being can choose to work.
Choose to create.
Choose to study.
Choose to care.
Choose to contemplate.
Choose to appreciate.
Choose to build.
Choose to rest.
Choose to do nothing.

The freedom to choose among these possibilities is not an absence of civilization.
It may be one of civilization’s highest purposes.

The Cushion therefore does not change human nature by force.
It shifts the floor.

It constructs an architecture in which the transaction fees of existence are progressively paid in advance, so that survival no longer has to be purchased anew every morning through compulsory economic performance.

The objective is not to abolish Doing.
It is to make Doing voluntary again.

Not because humanity has become perfect, but because the conditions that continuously force human beings into survival optimization have begun to recede.

When the floor changes, the human being does not become a different species.

The same human capacities remain: desire, curiosity, ambition, affection, contemplation, creation, competition, cooperation, rest.

What changes is the degree to which survival must govern their expression.

Technological civilization has spent centuries asking how much more humanity can produce.

The post-labor question is different:

**How much unnecessary friction can civilization finally remove so that humanity can decide for itself what is worth doing?**

That is the purpose of the Cushion.

Not to optimize humanity.
Not to abolish Doing.
But to make Being materially possible—and let whatever comes next emerge from there.
